# Introductory Philosophy

This section primarily acts as an introduction to the underlying philosophy of the text.

The text is an attempt to formalise my understanding of my studies into mathemathics, particularly in algebra. As I write this, it aims to cover the basics of abstract algebra which consits atleast of group and ring theory. I do not put an upper limit to the coverage as I expect to keep contributing to it as I learn further algebra.

This text isn't just a collection of notes, but rather an unconventional presentation of the topics usually taught. The text draws inspiration from Aluffi's "Algebra Chapter: 0"[Alu09] in the sense that it aims to introduce the structures using the language of category theory. Yet, this text goes even further by rearranging the way topics are usually introduced. The text emphasises on an intrensic structural/categorical understanding of algebraic objects instead of just generalisations of physical phenomenon such as "symmetries" or "arithemethic". For instance, a group is not introduced as a mathematical formulation of symmetry, but as an algebraic structure whose definition, properties, and relationships with other structures warrant investigation in their own right. The development is thus guided primarily by structural questions and the constructions they motivate, rather than by the particular phenomena these structures may represent. Hence in this way, it aims to treat algebraic objects as an end to itself. 

By doing this, I aim to accompalish two things:

1) Present an unconventional understanding of basic abstract algebra through the lens of category theory i.e. the way I have ended up seeing algebra because of my studies

2) From personal experience, I have found significat topics in algebra, such as normality of groups, end up feeling like clever tricks that have been pulled out from a hat. Through this uncoventional ways, I aim to show that such "tricks" aren't arbitrary but rather are forced to be exactly that way because of our assumptions i.e. the categorical structure itself forces such "tricks" as answers to the questions we find interesting

Hence, The reader must expect me to diverge and expand largely from a conventional undergraduate algebra textbook such as Dummit and Foote [DF04]. Though the broader flow from groups to rings would be the same, the topics in them would be largely different. 

The idea for each algebraic object, such as a "group", is to first understand it categorically and ask what would "structures" like products, coproducts, epimorphisms, monomorphisms etc would look like if the categorical object was that specific algebraic object. In this way, the first half of discussing an object would be largely structural. The latter part would consist of dealing with classical theory of the object, such as the combinatorial aspects of groups. 

I hope that the text would end up helping anyone who reads this in some way or the other. Thank you for your time.
